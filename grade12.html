Review of Derivatives: The Product Rule

The product rule for derivatives is Dₓ(f ● g)(x) = f(x) ● g'(x) + g(x) ● f'(x). We derive this formula from the limit of the difference quotient for (f ● g)(x). Review of Derivatives: The Quotient Rule

The derivative of f(x)/g(x) is equal to [g(x) ● f'(x) - f(x) ● g'(x)] / [g(x)]^2. A common mnemonic to remember the formula is "low de-high minus high de-low, over low-low", where "high" is the function in the numerator f(x) and "low" is the function in the denominator g(x). Integration by Parts3 videos

Integration by parts

The formula for integration by parts is ∫ u dv = uv - ∫ v du. The goal in using this method is to reduce a more complex integral, ∫ u dv, into a less complex integral, ∫ v du. The formula is derived from the product rule of differentiation. In using this method, we should decide what factor of the given integral should be assigned as u, with the remaining factors and the differential dx making up dv. The mnemonic LIATE can be used in choosing a good factor for u. We can follow this list in order of preference of choosing the factor to be assigned to u: Logarithmic functions Inverse trigonometric functions Algebraic functions Trigonometric functions Exponential functions This video is part of a lesson series in Integral #Calculus for my Grade 12 students in the Philippine Science High School Main Campus. Solids of revolution using disks and washers

Let's move to three dimensions! By rotating a two-dimensional region about an axis of rotation we can generate a solid of revolution. In the Cartesian plane, we can find the area of a region by summing the approximating areas and taking the limit as the number of approximating areas approach infinity. An analogous approach is taken in finding the volume of a solid of revolution by allowing the approximating area to generate a disk or washer, and summing up the volumes of these cylinders. Arc Length3 videos

Measuring the arc length

We can approximate the length of a smooth curve or the arc length by constructing line segments connecting points along the curve and taking the sum of the lengths of these line segments. Using calculus (more specifically, limits), we can find the exact length by taking the limit as the number of line segments (or subintervals) approach infinity. In this video we derive the formula for finding the length of a arc bound by an interval where the function is smooth in the said interval.
